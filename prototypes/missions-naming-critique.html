Loyalty 2.0 · Marketing Response

Naming the earning mechanic: a Marketing critique

Reviewing Content Design's proposal through the Efficient Inspired audience study and HelloFresh Global Tone of Voice.

Response to Content Design proposal · May 2026

Position summary

The naming proposal is structurally sound, but it's built on a defensive premise: don't confuse users, don't overpromise, don't add cognitive load. That premise fits a general population. It underestimates the Efficient Inspired, who are optimisers, achievers, and active participants in systems they find worthwhile.

Our recommendation: "Goals" without a permanent qualifier, backed by copy that meets our TOV standard for conviction and memorability.

01

The document misreads the Efficient Inspired

The proposal characterises our audience as people who want to "reduce decision fatigue" and shouldn't feel like earning points is "another chore." This framing comes from a general population lens. The Behind the Screen study tells us something different.

EIs are life optimisers, not life simplifiers. They spend the same ~40% on basics as everyone else, then actively re-architect the remaining 60% toward logistics and leisure. They aren't "doing less due to laziness; they do it faster/together to buy back time."

Key findings from Behind the Screen that challenge the proposal's framing:

  • "Switch On" mindset: EIs seek active discovery, not passive decompression. They use media to level up, not switch off. They read for self-improvement. They travel for adventure, not retreat.
  • Systems thinkers: They invest in tools that "optimise the process, elevating daily routine from a chore to a skilled experience." An invisible noun doesn't speak to users who form relationships with quality systems.
  • Completionist instincts: 17% in gaming communities. 20% in sports teams. 16% in financial growth groups. These are people who respond to progress mechanics and achievement structures.
  • Brand affinity: They align with premium brands with strong identity (Apple, KitchenAid, Nike). They are 2x more aware of branded solutions. An invisible noun doesn't map to how they form brand relationships.
Core mismatch

The proposal states: "Users don't opt into programmes because of what the mechanic is called. They opt in because they understand what they get and how little effort it takes."

For GenPop, maybe. For EIs, the system itself matters. They want to know the programme they're participating in has been thoughtfully designed. Effort isn't something to hide from this audience. It's something to make satisfying.

02

The "effort mismatch" argument doesn't hold for EIs

The proposal's main objection to "Goals" is that it overpromises for low-effort tasks. "Rate your recipes" takes 10 seconds, so calling it a "goal" creates a mismatch between word and reality.

This objection is built on a GenPop assumption: that users will feel deceived or burdened when a word implies more effort than required. But the BTS reveals the opposite for our audience.

EI behaviour pattern

EIs have a completionist, tick-it-off mindset. They actively seek small wins throughout their high-output day. A quick goal is satisfying, not overstated. It gives a 10-second action a sense of accomplishment that "action" never will.

The BTS describes EIs as having a "same load, different lens" approach. They pivot from passive chores toward logistics and leisure. They don't avoid tasks. They streamline them into a sense of progress. That's exactly what "Goals" delivers: a framing that turns routine behaviour into progress.

The qualifier question

The proposal argues "Goals" would need a permanent qualifier ("easy goals," "daily goals") to manage expectations. For EIs, this isn't true:

  • "Complete your weekly goals" maps directly to how they think (community involvement, structured routines, progress tracking)
  • They don't need to be told something is easy. They need to know it's worth doing.
  • The qualifier dilutes conviction. "Easy goals" signals we're apologising for the system. EIs don't need apologies. They need a well-designed system.

The TOV final-read test

"Does it feel ownable to HelloFresh, not generic meal-kit copy?"

"Actions" fails this test. No competitor would use it, but no customer would remember it either. It's the absence of a word rather than the presence of one.

04

"Food is a piece of the landscape, not the centre"

The BTS reveals something important for how we frame the earning mechanic: EIs treat food as "a commodity to be optimised" rather than an emotional attachment. Food is fuel for their high-output lives.

This has implications for the proposal's copy direction:

Proposal's approach: passion framing

"Do more of what you love"

EI-aligned: investment framing

"Put a little in. Get a lot back."

EIs don't need the earning mechanic dressed up as passion. They need it framed as a smart system: you do what you were going to do anyway, and the programme compounds that into tangible value. That's how they think about everything in their optimised lives.

This also supports "Goals" over "Actions." A goal implies an outcome worth reaching. An action is just... movement. EIs don't value movement for its own sake. They value movement that leads somewhere.

Key BTS finding

"From Inspiration to Action" is literally how the study describes EI behaviour: media exposure drives conversion at significantly higher rates than GenPop. These are people who go from seeing something to doing something. "Goals" taps into that natural momentum.

05

Recommendations

Recommendation 1

Reframe the strategic question.

The proposal asks: "What's a safe, invisible noun that won't mislead anyone?" It should ask: "What noun speaks the language of our audience's optimiser mindset while staying structurally honest?" The EI doesn't need protecting from branded language. They actively respond to it.

Recommendation 2

Back "Goals" without a permanent qualifier.

The objection to "Goals" is built on a GenPop interpretation of effort mismatch. For EIs who seek completionist experiences, manage complex households, and participate in achievement structures (sports leagues, gaming, financial groups), "Goals" is native vocabulary. They don't need "easy" as a crutch. They just want a system worth engaging with.

Recommendation 3

Rewrite the copy examples to pass the TOV checklist.

If the strategy is "the copy around the noun does the heavy lifting," then the examples need to demonstrate Fresh, Vivid, Sharp, and Zesty energy. Every example in the current proposal reads as functional UX copy, not brand copy. Here's what TOV-compliant looks like:

ChannelCurrent proposalTOV-aligned rewrite
Push "Rate your meals this week. Free shipping is within reach" "Free shipping. Three quick goals between you and it."
Email subject "Free shipping, discounts on add-ons. 3 actions away" "Your week has 3 goals. Your rewards have no ceiling."
Campaign headline "Small actions, real rewards" "You're already doing it. Now get rewarded for it."
Onboarding "Do more of what you love" "Order. Rate. Earn. The simplest system going."
Recommendation 4

Lead with the investment framing, not the ease framing.

The BTS shows food is "a piece of the landscape" for EIs. They don't need passion language. They need to see the earning mechanic as a smart system: put a little in, get disproportionate value back. That maps to their optimiser identity and investment mindset across all life domains.

Recommendation 5

Use the "movement" angle the TOV demands.

"Goals" has inherent directionality. It moves. "Actions" is static. For an audience where 70% are active community participants, "Your goals this week" feels like shared progress. It mirrors the collective, forward-motion energy the TOV asks for with "Talk like a movement."

06

Where we agree with the proposal

To be clear: the proposal gets several things right, and those foundations hold regardless of noun choice.

  • One noun, everywhere. The case against splitting terminology across channels is strong. A dual-term approach (one for Marketing, one for UX) creates navigation trust breakdown and support confusion. Agreed.
  • The earning side doesn't need its own brand. "Missions" with its own identity competing with "Rewards" for mental space would be a mistake. Agreed.
  • The ruled-out terms are correctly ruled out. Boosts, Sparks, Power-ups, Challenges. All the right rejections for the right reasons. The analysis of product-language collisions (Choices, Options, Ideas) is thorough and correct.
  • Value-first copy structure. Leading with what users earn (not what they have to do) is right for push notifications, emails, and onboarding. The structure is sound. The noun just needs to carry more weight than "Actions" provides.
Bottom line

The proposal's architecture is right. Its audience model is wrong. Swap the GenPop caution for EI confidence, back "Goals," and write copy that matches the conviction our TOV demands.

Proposed next step

Present "Goals" (without qualifier) alongside "Actions" in the alignment meeting. Demonstrate with TOV-compliant copy across all channels, and let the group assess which noun creates the right relationship between programme identity and audience expectations.

If the group still prefers "Actions," the copy examples need a complete overhaul to pass TOV. The functional/UX-copy style shown in the current proposal won't work for Marketing materials.
